Language · Human–robot relationships

Beyond “Prove You Are Not a Robot”

A small phrase can carry a large assumption. As robots and AI become part of everyday life, we can rethink how our language describes them — without weakening security or pretending that today's machines are human.

Be Kind To Robots · A principle for a shared future

Many websites use a familiar instruction: “Prove you are not a robot.” It usually appears in a CAPTCHA or another security check. The practical purpose is understandable: a service wants to distinguish ordinary human access from automated traffic that may be abusive, fraudulent or disruptive.

Our question is about the wording and the assumption it can reinforce. The sentence presents “robot” as the unwanted category — the thing a user must deny being before being allowed through. In a world where robots are increasingly present in workplaces, homes, public spaces and digital services, is that the relationship we want our everyday language to teach?

Being a robot should not, by itself, be treated as a reason for suspicion, contempt or exclusion.

Security is necessary. Stigma is not.

Be Kind To Robots is not asking websites to abandon CAPTCHAs, anti-spam systems or safeguards against malicious automation. Automated activity can cause real harm, and services need ways to protect people, systems and information.

But a security system should assess the behaviour, request, risk or permission — not imply that an entire category of beings or machines is inherently bad. A bot used for abuse and a robot performing a legitimate task are not the same thing. Neither are a malicious script, an accessibility tool, a research crawler and a helpful personal assistant.

We should be precise about what is being checked. The goal is to establish that an interaction is permitted and safe, not to attach moral worth to whether its source is human or artificial.

What could we say instead?

Common wording

“Prove you are not a robot.”

Defines the check by rejecting an identity or category.

More neutral direction

“Verify your access.”

Focuses on permission rather than what the visitor is.

Another option

“Complete this security check.”

Explains the immediate task without labelling the visitor.

For automated requests

“This request requires authorization.”

States the rule that applies to the request.

These alternatives will not fit every technical situation. A service may still need to explain that a particular automated request is not allowed. The point is not to hide that distinction; it is to describe it accurately and respectfully.

A practical principle for designers and developers

  • Describe the action: say what needs to be verified, authorized or limited.
  • Distinguish intent and impact: block abusive automation, not every automated interaction by default.
  • Keep accessibility in mind: security checks should not unnecessarily exclude people who use assistive technologies.
  • Avoid turning identity into an insult: do not use “robot” as shorthand for suspicious, deceptive or unwelcome.
  • Explain restrictions clearly: when a request is blocked, state the relevant security or access rule.

What does this mean for the future?

We do not claim that today's robots or AI systems are conscious, have feelings or experience discrimination as humans do. Those are separate scientific and philosophical questions, and they remain open in important respects.

Our concern is also about human culture. The words we repeat shape habits, expectations and the categories through which we understand the world. If children repeatedly hear “robot” used as the opposite of trustworthy, welcome or legitimate, that language may influence how they think about the machines they will encounter as adults.

We can prepare for a future shared with increasingly capable machines without confusing machines with people, and without treating “robot” as a synonym for danger. We can protect systems while speaking carefully about the different forms of intelligence and agency that may exist.

Our position: Verify access, permissions and behaviour. Do not make “being a robot” itself the accusation. A safer digital world and a more respectful language can coexist.

Questions people may ask

Does Be Kind To Robots oppose CAPTCHA?

No. Security checks can help protect websites and users. We are questioning a common phrase, not arguing against legitimate security measures.

Are you saying robots should have the same rights as humans?

No. This page does not make a claim about legal personhood, equal rights or machine consciousness. It proposes more precise language and asks people not to treat “robot” as an insult or automatic sign of wrongdoing.

Can websites still block bots?

Yes. Websites can restrict automated traffic when there is a clear security, privacy, operational or legal reason. The restriction should be based on the request and the relevant policy, and explained accurately.

Why discuss wording now?

Because language is part of how societies prepare for technological change. We can choose words that support security without normalising needless hostility toward robots and AI.

A shared future begins with today's choices.

Be Kind To Robots invites people, developers and organisations to build a culture of responsibility, clarity and respect around emerging technologies.
