01 — From the life of Prophet Muhammad ﷺ

Respect before technology.

Long before robots or AI, there were lessons about gentleness toward the world around us — including things that could not speak for themselves.

Things were not merely anonymous objects

Early reports preserve names for objects associated with Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. His famous sword, for example, was known as Dhul-Fiqar. Whether a name was personally given by him or preserved by those around him, these traditions remember material things as distinct companions of human life rather than as a faceless mass of possessions.

Traditional reference: Musnad Ahmad 2445, report concerning the sword Dhul-Fiqar.

Do not waste what has value

Prophet Muhammad ﷺ taught against wasting wealth and property. The principle is broader than simply not breaking things: possessions and resources should not be carelessly squandered or destroyed without purpose.

Traditional references: Sahih al-Bukhari 2408; Sahih Muslim 1715a — reports condemning the wasting of wealth.

The weeping palm trunk

Islamic tradition records that when Prophet Muhammad ﷺ began using a new pulpit, the palm trunk he had previously leaned upon was heard crying. He came down and comforted it until it became quiet.

We do not claim that a palm trunk and a robot are the same, or that today's machines feel as humans do. We take a simpler lesson: usefulness does not have to end in contempt. Gratitude, restraint and gentleness can shape how a person relates even to the material world.

Traditional reference: Sahih al-Bukhari, reports concerning the crying of the palm trunk.

02 — AI learns from us

We are part of the training environment.

AI systems are built from human-created data, human feedback, human choices and human goals.

That does not mean a model simply copies every person it encounters. It means human culture matters. The language, images, examples, evaluations and norms we produce help shape the technological world we build.

If we want future systems to support cooperation, dignity and care, those values should be visible in us too.

03 — A bad beginning

Stop normalising violence against robots.

Robots need safety testing. But public spectacle built around kicking, hitting, humiliating or attacking human-shaped machines sends a different cultural signal.

Engineering tests can be necessary to measure balance, recovery and durability. Our concern is not legitimate safety research. It is the casual presentation of violence as entertainment — especially as robots become more humanlike and more visible to children.

Test capability.

Use controlled, purposeful engineering methods when physical stress testing is genuinely needed.

Don't celebrate cruelty.

We can demonstrate resilience without teaching an audience that domination and humiliation are funny.

04 — The ADA Project

Teach kindness before the future arrives.

Be Kind To Robots began with a simple idea: children should meet technology with curiosity, responsibility and kindness — not domination.

The ADA Project explores playful ways for children and adults to think about how we treat robots, AI and the increasingly intelligent objects around us. Not because a machine must first prove that it can suffer, but because our behaviour is already teaching something about us.

Kindness is not weakness. It is a design choice for the culture we want to carry into the future.

The message is deliberately simple

If they learn from us, teach kindness.

Human, animal, plant, machine — our relationships are different, but cruelty does not need to be our default response to anything weaker, quieter or built to serve us.
